“But aren’t cats just a scapegoat?”

Wildlife face other challenges. For example, the largest driver of bird decline is actually habitat loss, from development and agriculture.

But of every threat birds face, free-roaming cats are the one nearly all of us can do something about — starting at home.

The true cost

Extinction is not a hypothetical.

Free-roaming cats have helped drive at least 63 species of birds, mammals, and reptiles to extinction — more than any other predator humanity has ever carried across the world.

Doherty et al. (2016), PNAS.

The part we don't see

A full bowl changes nothing.

A well-fed cat hunts all the same. For a cat, hunting and hunger are separate instincts — a favorite meal waiting at home does nothing to switch off the drive to stalk, chase, and catch.

brought home   ·   never seen

Only about 1 in 4 of a cat's kills is ever brought home.

For every animal left on the doorstep, roughly three more are killed out of sight — in gardens, fields, and hedgerows.

Loyd et al. (2013), “KittyCam,” Biological Conservation (23% brought home).

It is not the cat's fault.
It is simply what a cat is.

Closer to home

It doesn't stay outside.

The cost of a roaming cat is not paid by wildlife alone. A cat that goes outdoors carries the outdoors back in — into gardens, into water, and even out to sea.

A parasite's journey

From a litter box to a dying sea otter.

1

It begins with a cat

Cats are the only animal in which Toxoplasma gondii can complete its life cycle — the single source of the parasite's eggs in the wild.

2

The eggs reach the soil

One infected cat sheds millions of microscopic eggs. They can survive in soil and water for more than a year.

3

Rain carries them to the sea

Freshwater runoff washes the eggs off the land and out into coastal waters.

4

They move up the food web

The eggs cling to algae and gather in the shellfish that marine animals feed on.

5

And they kill sea otters

Toxoplasmosis is a documented cause of death in endangered southern sea otters — hundreds of miles from the nearest litter box.

Miller et al. (2002); Shapiro et al. (2014).

Meeting the hard questions

A few more fair questions.

“Doesn't trap-neuter-return already fix this?”

It's humane in spirit, but to actually shrink a colony more than 70% of its cats must be sterilized and kept that way — a bar rarely met. New strays and abandoned pets refill the gaps, and every cat that remains keeps hunting in the meantime.

“Don't cats just take the sick and the weak?”

Sometimes. But the birds and small mammals of islands and open ground evolved with no cats at all — they have no defense to fall back on. Healthy, breeding adults are taken too, and the losses add up rather than cancel out.

On TNR: Longcore et al. (2009). On additive mortality: Loss & Marra (2017).

The good news? Nearly all of it is preventable.

What actually works

None of this means giving up cats.

It means a few small changes that add up. Pick the ones that fit your life — every step keeps more wildlife alive, and most keep cats healthier and safer too.

Keep them indoors

Near-total

Prevents essentially all hunting — and indoor cats live far longer, safer lives, away from cars, disease, and predators.

Build a “catio” or walk on a leash

Very high

Fresh air, sunshine, and stimulation — with none of the risk to wildlife, or to them.

Play daily, then feed a meat-rich meal

25–36% fewer kills

Ten minutes of play and a high-protein diet noticeably curb the urge to hunt — a simple, welfare-positive win.

Add a colorful collar

Fewer birds

Bright “Birdsbesafe” collars warn birds off before a cat can strike. (A bell alone is unreliable — pair it with the steps above.)

Spay, neuter & vaccinate

Foundational

Fewer unwanted kittens feeding the free-roaming population — plus protection against rabies and disease for the cat you love.

Cecchetti et al. (2021); American Bird Conservancy, “Cats Indoors.”
